# EU, EEA, and UK

Verified: 2026-11-22. Check items marked **(time-sensitive)** against a primary source before you rely on them.

Primary sources: GDPR text (eur-lex.europa.eu, Regulation (EU) 2016/679), EDPB guidelines (edpb.europa.eu), UK GDPR and Data Protection Act 2018 (legislation.gov.uk), ICO guidance (ico.org.uk).

## When it applies

The GDPR (Art. 3) applies to a business that:

- is established in the EU or EEA, for processing in the context of that establishment, or
- is not established there but offers goods or services to people in the EU or EEA, or monitors their behaviour there (for example, tracking or analytics).

The UK GDPR applies in the same way for the UK. A non-EU business that the GDPR covers under Art. 3(2) must usually appoint a representative in the EU (Art. 27). The UK GDPR has the same rule for a UK representative. Ask the user if they have one; do not assume.

## Privacy policy: required information (Art. 13 and Art. 14)

When the business collects data from the person (Art. 13), the policy must give:

- Identity and contact details of the controller, and of the representative if any.
- DPO contact details, if a DPO is appointed.
- Each purpose and its lawful basis (Art. 6). Name the basis per purpose. Do not list all bases without linking them to purposes.
- The legitimate interests, where the basis is legitimate interests.
- Recipients or categories of recipients.
- Transfers outside the EEA or UK, and the safeguard used.
- Retention period, or the criteria that decide it.
- The rights: access, rectification, erasure, restriction, objection, and portability.
- The right to withdraw consent at any time, where the basis is consent.
- The right to complain to a supervisory authority.
- Whether the data is a statutory or contractual requirement, whether the person must give it, and the consequences if they do not.
- Automated decision-making, including profiling (Art. 22): meaningful information about the logic, its significance, and its likely consequences.

When the data comes from another source (Art. 14), also give the categories of data and the source.

## Lawful bases that SaaS products commonly use

| Purpose | Usual basis |
|---|---|
| Provide the service, manage the account, take payment | Contract, Art. 6(1)(b) |
| Tax and accounting records, legal requests | Legal obligation, Art. 6(1)(c) |
| Security, fraud prevention, basic product analytics | Legitimate interests, Art. 6(1)(f) |
| Marketing email to non-customers, non-essential cookies, special category data | Consent, Art. 6(1)(a) (and Art. 9(2)(a) for special categories) |

These are typical choices, not rules. The business must decide the basis for its own processing.

## Rights and response times

- Respond without undue delay and within **one month** of receipt. Extend by two further months for complex or numerous requests, with notice in the first month (Art. 12(3)). Write "one month", not "30 days".
- Requests are free, except for manifestly unfounded or excessive requests.
- The objection to direct marketing is absolute (Art. 21(3)).

## Children (Art. 8)

For information society services offered directly to a child on the basis of consent, parental consent is needed below 16. Member states can set a lower age, down to 13. The UK age is 13. The UK Age Appropriate Design Code (Children's Code) applies to online services likely to be accessed by under-18s in the UK.

## International transfers (Chapter V)

Give the mechanism for each transfer outside the EEA:

- **Adequacy decision**: the destination has an EU adequacy decision. For the US, adequacy covers only organizations certified under the EU-US Data Privacy Framework (DPF). Check certification at dataprivacyframework.gov.
- **Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs)**: Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2021/914. Most providers include them in their data processing agreement.
- **Binding Corporate Rules**, or Art. 49 derogations for limited cases.

**(time-sensitive)** The EU General Court upheld the DPF on 3 September 2025 (Case T-553/23, Latombe). An appeal to the Court of Justice is pending (Case C-703/25 P). If the Court of Justice invalidates the DPF, transfers that rely on it need SCCs. For the UK, the UK-US "data bridge" (UK Extension to the DPF) and the UK International Data Transfer Agreement (IDTA) or UK Addendum to the SCCs apply.

## Cookies and similar technologies

- **EU**: ePrivacy Directive Art. 5(3), as transposed in each member state. Storing or reading information on a device needs prior consent, except where it is strictly necessary for the service the user asked for. Analytics, advertising, and session replay need consent. Consent must be a clear affirmative act: no pre-ticked boxes (CJEU C-673/17, Planet49). Rejecting must be as easy as accepting under most regulators' guidance.
- **UK**: PECR regulation 6. **(time-sensitive)** The Data (Use and Access) Act 2025 added exemptions from consent for some low-risk purposes, such as some analytics, from 5 February 2026. The user must still get clear information and a simple way to object. Advertising cookies still need consent. Check the current ICO guidance before you rely on an exemption.
- The policy must list cookie categories, purposes, the third parties that set them, and durations, and explain how to change choices.

## UK-specific changes (Data (Use and Access) Act 2025)

**(time-sensitive)** Most changes are in force as of 2026. Two affect the policy:

- Controllers must make it easy to submit data protection complaints to them (for example, with an electronic form), acknowledge each complaint, and respond without undue delay. These provisions are reported to apply from 19 June 2026; check the commencement regulations and ICO guidance for the exact duties. Describe the complaint process in the policy, and keep the right to complain to the ICO.
- New recognized legitimate interests and changes to automated decision-making rules. Check current ICO guidance before you draft text that relies on them.

## Consumer terms (EU and UK)

- **Unfair terms**: EU Directive 93/13/EEC and UK Consumer Rights Act 2015 Part 2. A term that is not transparent, or that creates a significant imbalance against the consumer, does not bind the consumer. Broad liability exclusions, unilateral changes without notice or a right to cancel, and broad indemnities are common problems.
- **Mandatory law**: a choice of law does not take away the protection of mandatory consumer law of the consumer's country of habitual residence (Rome I Regulation Art. 6). EU consumers can usually sue in their home courts (Brussels I bis Art. 18). Do not draft an exclusive foreign jurisdiction clause for EU consumers.
- **Withdrawal right**: consumers have a 14-day right to withdraw from distance contracts (Consumer Rights Directive 2011/83/EU; UK Consumer Contracts Regulations 2013). For digital services and digital content, the right can end earlier only if the consumer expressly asked for immediate performance and acknowledged the loss of the right. The terms must describe the withdrawal right and the model withdrawal form information.
- **(time-sensitive) EU withdrawal function**: Directive (EU) 2023/2673 added Art. 11a to the Consumer Rights Directive. From 19 June 2026, traders that conclude contracts through an online interface must offer a clearly labelled withdrawal function ("withdraw from contract here" or equivalent) with a confirmation step. Some member states transposed it late. This is a product change: add it to the publishing checklist.
- **UK liability**: a trader cannot exclude liability for death or personal injury caused by negligence (Consumer Rights Act 2015 s. 65).
- **(time-sensitive) UK subscriptions**: the Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024 subscription contract rules (clear pre-contract information, reminder notices, easy exit) are expected to start in January 2027. Check the commencement regulations.
- **Online Dispute Resolution**: the EU ODR platform closed on 20 July 2025 (Regulation (EU) 2024/3228). Remove links to it. Do not add new ones.

## EU Digital Services Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065)

If the product hosts content from users (an intermediary or hosting service), the terms must describe in clear language any restrictions on content and the content moderation process, including algorithmic moderation and the internal complaint process (Art. 14). Hosting services must have a notice mechanism for illegal content (Art. 16) and must give reasons when they restrict content (Art. 17). Online platforms have more duties. Micro and small enterprises are exempt from some platform duties, but not from Art. 14.

## EU AI Act transparency (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Art. 50)

**(time-sensitive)** Art. 50 applies from 2 August 2026:

- AI systems that interact with people (for example, chatbots) must tell people that they are interacting with an AI, unless this is obvious.
- Providers of generative AI must mark output as AI-generated in a machine-readable format. The Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744) gave systems on the market before 2 August 2026 until 2 December 2026 for this marking duty.
- Deployers of deep fakes must disclose that the content is artificially generated or manipulated.

These are mostly product duties. Mention them in the terms or policy where they help, and add the product changes to the publishing checklist.

## Text you can adapt

```markdown
### If you are in the EEA or the UK

[LEGAL_ENTITY_NAME] is the controller of your personal data. We use your personal data on these legal bases:

- **Contract** (Art. 6(1)(b) GDPR): to provide the service you signed up for.
- **Legitimate interests** (Art. 6(1)(f) GDPR): to keep the service secure and to understand how it is used. You can object to this use.
- **Consent** (Art. 6(1)(a) GDPR): for [marketing emails / non-essential cookies]. You can withdraw consent at any time.
- **Legal obligation** (Art. 6(1)(c) GDPR): to keep tax and accounting records.

You have the right to access, correct, delete, restrict, and port your personal data, and to object to some uses. Contact us at [PRIVACY_EMAIL]. We reply within one month.

You can complain to your local data protection authority. In the UK, this is the Information Commissioner's Office (ico.org.uk). In the EU, see the list of authorities at edpb.europa.eu.
```
