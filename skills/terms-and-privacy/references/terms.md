# Terms of service sections

Use these sections as the baseline. Leave out sections that do not apply (for example, payments for a free product). Consumer law limits what terms can say: check each clause against the jurisdiction files.

## 1. Introduction and acceptance

- Who the contract is with (legal entity name and address) and which products it covers.
- How the user agrees: account creation, a checkbox, or use of the service. An unticked checkbox with a link to the terms ("clickwrap") is more enforceable than a footer link alone ("browsewrap").
- Minimum age. If a person accepts on behalf of an organization, they confirm that they have authority.
- Links to the privacy policy and any other policies that form part of the terms (acceptable use, API terms).

## 2. Definitions

Define only terms that the document uses: "Service", "Account", "Content" or "User Content", "Customer", "Subscription", "Output" (for AI features). Use the same terms as the privacy policy.

## 3. Accounts

- Accurate registration information.
- The user keeps credentials secure and is responsible for activity on the account.
- For team or workspace products: who owns the account and its content (the organization or the person), and what admins can see.

## 4. Acceptable use

- Prohibited conduct: illegal use, infringement, harassment, malware, spam, unauthorized access, scraping or automated access without permission, reverse engineering (subject to law that allows it), reselling without permission, and abuse of free tiers.
- Product-specific limits: rate limits, storage, fair use.
- For AI features: no use to generate unlawful content, no use to build competing models if the user wants that restriction, and compliance with the AI providers' usage policies where those pass through.
- Consequences: removal of content, suspension, termination.
- A long list can go in a separate Acceptable Use Policy.

## 5. User content

- The user keeps ownership of their content.
- The user grants the business a licence limited to what it needs to host, process, display, and operate the service. Make the scope match the privacy policy: do not take a licence to use content for AI training or marketing unless the business does that and the privacy policy says so.
- The user confirms that they have the rights to the content they submit.
- Moderation and complaints: how to report content, how the business reviews reports. The EU Digital Services Act adds requirements (see `eu-uk.md`). US copyright notices: see the DMCA section in `us.md`.

## 6. AI output

Include if the product shows AI-generated output.

- Output can be inaccurate. The user is responsible for reviewing it before relying on it.
- Ownership of output, as far as the business and its providers can grant it.
- Output can be similar for different users.

## 7. Fees, subscriptions, and refunds

- Prices, billing period, taxes, and payment methods.
- Free trials: what happens when the trial ends, and how to cancel before the first charge.
- Automatic renewal: state it clearly, with the renewal period and how to cancel. Several laws set strict rules for automatic renewal and cancellation (see the jurisdiction files).
- Price changes: how much notice, and the right to cancel before the new price applies.
- Refunds: state the policy. Do not write a blanket "no refunds" clause where consumer law gives rights (Australia, EU, UK). Keep statutory rights intact.
- Failed payments and downgrade or suspension.

## 8. Intellectual property

- The business owns the service, software, and brand. Users get a limited, revocable, non-transferable licence to use the service.
- Feedback: the business may use feedback without obligation.
- Open-source components keep their own licences.

## 9. Third-party services

The service may link to or integrate third-party services. Their terms apply to them. The business is not responsible for them, subject to law that says otherwise.

## 10. Service changes and availability

The business can change or discontinue features. Give notice of material adverse changes for paid plans. Planned maintenance. Link to an SLA if one exists.

## 11. Termination

- The user can stop using the service and close the account at any time. State how.
- The business can suspend or terminate for breach, with notice where practical.
- Effects of termination: access ends, the data export window, deletion timeline (match the privacy policy), refunds for prepaid fees where required.
- Clauses that survive termination: IP, payment obligations, disclaimers, liability limits, indemnity, dispute resolution.

## 12. Disclaimers

The service is provided "as is" and "as available", to the extent the law allows. Add the jurisdiction carve-outs: Australian Consumer Law guarantees, EU and UK consumer statutory rights, and US state laws that do not allow some disclaimers.

## 13. Limitation of liability

- Exclude indirect and consequential loss, to the extent the law allows.
- Cap total liability (for example, fees paid in the 12 months before the claim, or a fixed amount for free users).
- Carve-outs that the law requires: death or personal injury caused by negligence, fraud, and liability that cannot be excluded by law. In the EU and UK, consumer terms that exclude liability too broadly are unfair and do not bind the consumer.

## 14. Indemnity

The user indemnifies the business for claims from the user's breach of the terms, the user's content, or the user's violation of law. For consumers, keep this narrow: broad indemnities against consumers can be unfair terms in the EU, UK, and Australia. Many consumer terms limit indemnity to business users.

## 15. Changes to these terms

How the business announces changes, the notice period for material changes, and what happens if the user does not agree (they can stop using the service and, for paid plans, cancel). Unilateral changes without notice can be unfair terms in the EU, UK, and Australia.

## 16. Governing law and disputes

- Governing law and courts. State them explicitly (for example, "the laws of New South Wales, Australia").
- A choice of law does not remove the protection of mandatory consumer law in the consumer's country (EU, UK). Say so for consumer terms.
- Informal resolution first (for example, 30 days of good-faith negotiation).
- US arbitration and class action waivers: see `us.md`. Do not apply them to EU, UK, or Australian consumers without a lawyer's review.
- EU consumers: there is no mandatory link to the EU Online Dispute Resolution platform any more; it closed in 2025. Do not add one.

## 17. General

Entire agreement, severability, no waiver, assignment (the business can assign in a merger or sale), force majeure, notices (how and where to send legal notices), and the language that prevails if there are translations.

## 18. Contact

Email and postal address for legal notices and complaints.
